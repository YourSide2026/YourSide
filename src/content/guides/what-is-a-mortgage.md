Buying a home is one of the biggest financial decisions you’ll make, and for most people it isn’t possible without a mortgage. If you’re new to it, the jargon can feel like a different language. This guide explains what a mortgage is, how it works in New Zealand and what you’ll need before you apply.

## What is a mortgage?

A mortgage is a loan used to buy property. You borrow money from a lender, usually a bank, and pay it back with interest over an agreed period, often up to 30 years.

What makes a mortgage different from other loans is **security**. The home you buy acts as security for the loan, and the lender registers its interest on the property’s title. If the loan isn’t repaid as agreed, the lender has the right to sell the property to recover what it’s owed. That security is why mortgage interest rates are usually much lower than rates on personal loans or credit cards.

Technically, the *mortgage* is the security agreement and the *home loan* is the money you borrow, but in everyday conversation the two words mean the same thing.

## How does a mortgage work?

There are three main parts to every home loan:

- **The principal:** the amount you borrow.
- **The interest:** what the lender charges you for borrowing, worked out as a percentage of what you still owe.
- **The term:** how long you have to pay it all back.

Each repayment covers that period’s interest plus a portion of the principal. Early on, more of each payment goes towards interest. As your balance drops, more goes towards the principal, so you pay the loan off faster over time.

### Table loans vs reducing loans

Most New Zealand home loans are **table loans**. Your repayments stay the same for as long as your interest rate does, which makes budgeting easier. With a **reducing loan**, you pay off a fixed amount of principal each time plus interest, so repayments start higher and fall over time. Reducing loans are less common, but they can save interest overall.

## What deposit do you need?

Your deposit is the part of the purchase price you pay yourself. In New Zealand, the Reserve Bank limits how much banks can lend to people with small deposits. As a general guide:

- **Owner-occupiers** usually need a deposit of **20%** of the purchase price.
- **Property investors** usually need **30%**.

Lenders can make a limited number of loans to buyers with smaller deposits, and some government-backed schemes help eligible first home buyers buy with as little as 5%. Those options have extra criteria and sometimes extra costs, so this is an area where advice really pays off.

Many first home buyers use their **KiwiSaver** savings towards a deposit. If you’ve been a member for at least three years, you can usually withdraw most of your balance to buy your first home.

## Fixed vs floating interest rates

You’ll choose how your interest rate works. You can also split your loan across more than one option.

### Fixed rate

Your rate is locked in for a set period, commonly between six months and five years. Your repayments are predictable, but if you want to repay a lot early or move lenders during the fixed term, you may have to pay a **break fee**.

### Floating rate

Your rate can go up or down at any time. Floating rates are usually higher than fixed rates, but you can make extra repayments or pay the loan off without break fees.

### Revolving credit and offset loans

These are flexible options that work a bit like a large overdraft or let your savings reduce the interest you pay. They can be powerful tools for disciplined borrowers, but they aren’t right for everyone.

Many people split their loan, for example fixing most of it and keeping a smaller part floating for extra repayments. You can compare current rates on our [interest rates page](/interest-rates/).

## How much can you borrow?

How much a lender will offer depends on your income, expenses, existing debts, deposit and the property you’re buying. Lenders also check that you could still afford repayments if rates rose, using a **test rate** that’s higher than today’s actual rates. Debt-to-income rules also cap most borrowing at a multiple of your income.

For a quick estimate, try our [borrowing calculator](/calculators/how-much-can-i-borrow/). Just remember that every lender assesses things a little differently.

## What you’ll need to apply

Having these ready makes the process faster:

- Photo ID and proof of address
- Recent payslips or, if you’re self-employed, financial statements
- Bank statements showing your income, spending and savings
- Details of any debts, including credit cards and buy now, pay later accounts
- Proof of your deposit, including KiwiSaver balances

## Pre-approval: know your budget before you buy

**Pre-approval** is a lender’s conditional agreement to lend you up to a set amount, subject to the property you choose. It means you know your price range before you start house-hunting, and it lets you move quickly when the right home comes along. Pre-approvals usually last a few months, so time it with your search.

## Other costs to budget for

Your deposit isn’t the only cost. Make sure you also allow for:

- Legal or conveyancing fees
- A building inspection and LIM report
- A registered valuation, if your lender asks for one
- Insurance, which is required before settlement
- Moving costs

## Frequently asked questions

### How long is a typical mortgage in New Zealand?

Most home loans are set up over 25 or 30 years, but you can pay them off faster with higher or extra repayments.

### Can I get a mortgage with less than a 20% deposit?

Sometimes, yes. Lenders have limited room to lend to buyers with low deposits, and some schemes help eligible first home buyers. An adviser can tell you whether you’re likely to qualify.

### What happens when my fixed rate ends?

Your lender will offer you new rates to *refix*. It’s a good time to review your options. You don’t have to take the first offer, and you may be able to switch lenders.
